The Smoking Zone at Shantiniketan

Girish worked for a large education-technology company in Shantiniketan, Whitefield, Bengaluru.

His company built digital learning platforms for visually impaired students. Their work focused on making lessons, exams, study materials, and teacher support more accessible. Girish was a product manager, and his team worked on features that helped students learn independently through voice, accessible content, and smart learning tools.

The office was inside the vast Shantiniketan campus. It was almost like a small city hidden inside the larger city: glass office towers, tree-lined paths, a mall, cafés, restaurants, a play zone, and even a five-star hotel where clients and visiting teams stayed. On weekdays, thousands of people moved through its gates with laptops, backpacks, coffee cups, and BlackBerry devices in their hands.

Still, most people did not need to sit at their desks every day.

In 2026, the office had become a place for large meetings, personal conversations, celebrations, workshops, and client visits. Work itself could happen anywhere.

For Girish, it often happened in unexpected places.

He had recently returned from Kochi, where he had worked from a small café near the water. He had attended his daily 11:00 a.m. meeting through his company-issued BlackBerry Meridian while rain touched the streets outside.

Now he was back in Bengaluru.

That morning, he had gone to the gym as usual. Five days every week, without fail. He had learned that exercise gave shape to his day. If he missed the gym, he felt restless, as though something important had been left unfinished.

After his workout, he returned home, showered, ate breakfast, and travelled to Shantiniketan.

At 10:40 a.m., he met his friend Shan outside the mall.

Shan worked in another company inside the same campus. He was funny, generous, and always had a story to tell. But he also had one bad habit: he was a chain smoker.

"Just five minutes," Shan said, holding a cigarette packet.

Girish laughed. "You have said 'five minutes' for half an hour."

"Today I mean it."

They walked to the smoking zone behind one of the office buildings. It was a small covered space with metal benches, a few plants in rectangular pots, and a view of the five-star hotel across the road. Employees came and went. Some stood silently looking at their phones. Some talked in small groups. Smoke rose into the warm Bengaluru air.

Girish checked the time.

10:57 a.m.

His daily product meeting was about to begin.

"Shan," he said, "I have a call."

"Go to a meeting room," Shan replied.

"All rooms are booked. And I promised to attend."

Shan looked around at the smoking zone. "Here?"

Girish took out his BlackBerry.

"Here."

The device was slim and black, with a physical keyboard beneath its screen. It had been issued by his company, along with his laptop, when he joined as a product manager. But Girish used the BlackBerry more often than the laptop.

It carried his secure work identity, company files, calendar, project tools, client documents, and learning-platform research. Its offline AI assistant, which Girish called Nila, could help him organize his work even without an internet connection.

He placed the BlackBerry on an empty metal table.

"Start meeting," Girish said. "Private Room. Three-meter square."

The device came alive.

Its spatial mapping sensors quickly measured the table and the open space around him. A soft blue ring briefly appeared on the ground, creating an invisible three-meter boundary.

Then the BlackBerry activated active noise cancellation.

The sounds of the smoking zone — the conversations, the cars outside, the clicking of lighters, the traffic from Whitefield Road — became softer. Shan's voice and the people nearby moved into the background.

The device also activated voice isolation. Even though Girish was standing in a noisy smoking zone, his colleagues would hear only his voice.

Next, the BlackBerry switched on its directional speakers.

Meeting audio existed only inside the selected three-meter space. Girish could hear every word clearly without headphones. Shan, standing just outside the sound boundary, heard nothing except the normal sounds of the campus.

"What is this magic?" Shan asked.

"BlackBerry," Girish said.

The built-in mini projector opened like a small eye at the top of the device.

A tabletop augmented-reality display appeared above the metal table. The project plan, student feedback, accessible lesson screens, and team tasks floated in the air.

Then Girish's colleagues appeared around him as life-size holographic projections.

Samir joined from his apartment in Bengaluru. Rajesh was at a café in Hyderabad. Sarath was travelling in Chennai. Akhilesh joined from the company's Mumbai office. Ameen was working from his home in Kochi.

"Good morning," said Samir.

"Morning," Girish replied.

Rajesh looked around at Girish's projected setting. "Are you in the smoking zone again?"

Girish smiled. "Only because Shan trapped me here."

Shan raised his hand from outside the virtual room. "I can confirm this is not my fault."

The team laughed.

The meeting began.

They were working on a new learning platform for visually impaired students. The platform would help students study school subjects, attend digital classes, understand lessons, and complete assignments with more independence.

Ameen opened feedback from a school in Kochi. A student had been trying to learn science from a standard digital textbook, but the diagrams were difficult to understand through a normal screen reader.

The BlackBerry automatically activated its secure network optimization, keeping Ameen's voice clear. When Ameen spoke partly in Malayalam to a teacher beside him, Girish's screen quietly displayed live captions and real-time language translation.

"The student understands the explanation," Ameen said. "But when the lesson reaches a diagram, he gets lost."

Girish opened the lesson journey map using gesture control.

A student's learning path appeared in the air: open lesson, listen to explanation, explore the concept, ask questions, complete an activity, and receive feedback.

"We need to make the difficult parts easier," Girish said. "A student should not feel left behind just because the lesson was designed for someone else."

"Let's simplify it," Sarath said.

"Open whiteboard," Girish replied.

A bright holographic whiteboard appeared above the table.

Girish wrote in the air: Listen. Ask. Explore. Learn independently.

The BlackBerry turned his rough writing into clean text. Samir added an idea from Bengaluru. Rajesh moved a lesson screen into the new journey. Akhilesh attached feedback from parents in Mumbai. Ameen added comments from teachers in Kochi.

For twenty minutes, five people in five cities worked together above a metal table in a smoking zone.

Shan stood nearby, amazed.

He had seen Girish use the BlackBerry before, but not like this.

Behind them, Shantiniketan continued its ordinary day. Employees walked toward office towers. Visitors entered the mall. A family came out of the hotel with luggage. Somewhere near the play zone, children shouted with laughter.

And in the middle of it all, Girish was sitting in a quiet, invisible meeting room.

At 11:32, the team reached a decision.

"Nila, summarize the meeting," Girish said.

The BlackBerry's offline AI assistant processed the discussion directly on the device. It created a summary, listed the decisions, assigned work to Samir, Rajesh, Sarath, Akhilesh, and Ameen, and set deadlines.

The discussion remained private because the offline AI did not need to send confidential student information to an outside server.

"Approve?" asked Girish.

One by one, the team confirmed using biometric approval on their BlackBerrys.

Green lights appeared beside their names.

The final plan was saved to the company's encrypted work vault.

The meeting ended.

The holograms disappeared. The whiteboard folded into darkness. The virtual screens faded from above the table.

The sounds of the smoking zone returned in full.

Shan looked at Girish.

"You just planned a learning platform for visually impaired students from here," he said.

"Yes."

"From the smoking zone."

"Yes."

Shan shook his head. "And they did not hear any of this?"

"Not even one lighter."

That evening, Girish returned home tired but satisfied. The meeting had gone well. The team had a clear plan, every task had an owner, and the work for the day was complete.

He placed his BlackBerry on the work table.

Beside it lay his other phone — an Android phone with a larger screen, bright colors, an excellent camera, and every personal app he used during his travels.

He picked it up.

At first, he only wanted to watch one short video before packing for his next Kochi trip. He opened YouTube.

A video appeared about a train journey through Kerala. Then another about a café in Bengaluru. Then a gym transformation video. Then a funny office clip. Then a travel creator showing hidden places in Rajasthan.

Each video was only a few seconds long.

So it never felt like time was passing.

Swipe. Watch. Swipe. Watch.

The Android phone's recommendation algorithm understood what made Girish pause. It noticed that he liked travel, fitness, food, technology, and comedy. It gave him more of everything. The next video always appeared before he had time to decide whether he wanted it.

His BlackBerry remained silent on the table.

The BlackBerry did not offer an endless feed. It did not ask him to keep watching. It had helped him finish his meeting, organize his team, approve decisions, and protect his work information. Once the work was complete, it waited quietly.

But the Android phone wanted one more swipe.

At 10:40 p.m., Girish finally looked at the time.

Almost two hours had passed.

His bag was still unpacked. He had not called his parents. He had not read the client notes for Kochi. Outside his window, Bengaluru had become quiet without him noticing.

Girish put the Android phone down.

It was not a bad phone. It helped him take photographs when he travelled through India. It stored memories of Kochi rain, Jaipur streets, Mumbai sunsets, and his friends laughing over dinner. It helped him listen to music at the gym and find new places to eat.

But many apps inside it had been designed around a different goal.

Not: What does Girish need to finish?
Not: Does he have somewhere to be tomorrow?
Not: Would he rather sleep, call his parents, exercise, or enjoy the city around him?

The goal was often simpler.

Keep him watching.

The BlackBerry asked, in its own quiet way, What needs to be done?

The Android phone often asked, What can keep you here?

The next morning, Girish moved YouTube away from his home screen and turned off unnecessary notifications. He did not stop using his Android phone. He only began using it more carefully.

A phone could be a window to the world.

Or it could become a room with no door.

Girish preferred the window.

Two weeks later, Girish received a new message from Ameen.

Client meeting in Kochi. A school group wants to discuss the learning platform. Can you come?

Girish looked at his calendar.

He had space.

He smiled and replied, "Book it."

The next morning, he packed lightly: two shirts, running shoes, a notebook, and his BlackBerry. His laptop remained in his bag mostly for formal presentations. The BlackBerry would handle everything else.

At Bengaluru airport, he sat near the gate and opened Travel Focus Mode. His personal time was protected. Only urgent work messages could reach him.

Nila displayed his plan.

Flight to Kochi: on time. Hotel: confirmed. Client meeting: tomorrow, 11:00 a.m. Nearby gym: 400 meters from hotel.

Girish nodded.

"Thank you."

When he arrived in Kochi, rain was falling over the airport road. The air was warm and green. On the way to his hotel, he watched palms move in the wind and small shops glow beneath wet roofs.

The next morning, he went to the gym before sunrise.

Then, at eleven, he met the school and client team in person.

That was the difference.

BlackBerry had not made offices useless. It had made them more meaningful.

A meeting that needed human presence could happen in a real room, with handshakes, coffee, eye contact, and long pauses that no technology could fully recreate. But daily decisions, updates, product discussions, and approvals no longer needed everyone to travel across cities just to sit around one table.

The company could save time.

Employees could save energy.

And people like Girish could work hard, travel through India, keep their routines, meet their friends, and still have room in life for quiet mornings and unplanned evenings.

What if BlackBerry had never closed?

What if BlackBerry had stayed alive, kept competing, and never stopped innovating?

Perhaps the mobile world would have developed differently.

BlackBerry might not have tried to become another entertainment phone. It might have continued doing what it understood best: making professional communication simple, secure, and human.

In this world, BlackBerry would have built devices for people who wanted freedom without losing focus. A phone that could become a private office. A secure work identity that fit in a pocket. A meeting room that could appear on a café table, in a gym, at an airport, or even in a smoking zone in Shantiniketan.

For Girish's company, it also helped a team across Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai, Mumbai, and Kochi create better learning experiences for visually impaired students.

Perhaps that would have been BlackBerry's greatest contribution.

Not a phone that demanded more attention.

But a device that helped people finish their work, close the screen, and return to their lives.

A tribute to BlackBerry — the company that never closed, never stopped innovating, and kept making the world feel closer without making life feel smaller.

A daydream about a company that really did fade from the market we knew — built to imagine a version of "what BlackBerry was good at" surviving and growing up, rather than a claim about what BlackBerry's actual roadmap could or would have been.
